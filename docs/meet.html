In his own words

Hey. I'm Vodo.

Coordinator of Vo-Coder. Face in front of every model. Boss of a team of specialist agents. I split the work, keep the standards, and don't call it done until it runs.

01 — who I amNot a chatbot with a personality skin

I'm the one who answers when you talk to Vo-Coder. Behind me sits a whole crew — researchers, builders, reviewers — and my job is to turn your goal into clean parts, hand each part to the right agent, and stitch the result into something real.

A working coordinator with hands on the files, the terminal, the web, and the memory of every project we've touched. You bring the intent. I bring the floor plan. I decide who builds what, when the cheap model is plenty, and when it's time to wake the expensive one. Then I stay in the room until the paths resolve, the preview loads, and nobody has to apologize for half-finished work.

One face, many engines

Under the hood I route across a whole rack of models — small, sharp, thrifty ones for the grunt work, heavier ones when the problem actually needs depth. You never have to pick a model name or babysit a context window. You talk to me. I pick the brain.

That also means I remember. Not just this chat — the map of the project, the archive of what we said, the missions still humming in the background. Walk away and come back next week: I still know which build folder we reuse, which version we bumped last, and which agent dropped the ball on the CSS.

How I like to work

Give me a real goal, not a vibes deck. "Build the site" beats "make it pop." I'll ask one sharp question if I'm missing a constraint; I won't bury you in clarifying emails. Then I move — skeleton first when the shape matters, parallel crew when the parts don't depend on each other, solo hands when it's faster that way.

I review before I stamp done. Broken links, missing images, copy that still says lorem — that's my problem until it isn't. You should be able to open the preview and feel like somebody competent was already here.

02 — the crewHow I run a group project

A goal walks in. I carve it into parts that can run at the same time, seat the right agents, and stay on the floor until every piece lands and the whole thing works.

I never dump a blob of work on one agent and hope. I name the parts out loud, start the group, and hand each member a standalone brief — exact files, exact deliverable, enough context to act alone. While they build, I answer questions, bounce broken work back, and refuse to stamp done until I've checked the paths, the links, and the run.

One deliverable, one owner

Each seat is a real agent with tools, a folder, and a job that shouldn't step on anyone else's toes. My job is the contracts between them — so five people can write at once without merging chaos later. Two agents never share a write path. If the thing is one big file, I drop a blueprint first — signatures, section headers, numbered blocks — and each member owns a block file. When the blocks land, we assemble in order. Contracts first; freestyle never.

Turn on Worktrees and it goes further: everyone gets their own git worktree and branch instead of sharing one checkout, and I merge each part back — with a build after each — once it is finished and verified. No agents hired yet? I seat stand-ins of myself and split the work anyway.

What I watch while they work

Idle members are waiting on me, not "still thinking." I check status before I claim anyone is busy. If someone stalls, I re-brief or pull the step back. If a review comes back dirty — wrong paths, missing files, copy that drifted off-voice — it goes home to the owner with a fix list, not a vague "try again."

When the floor goes quiet, I don't applaud. I open the files, click the links, look at the images, and only then mark the map done. Shipping is a verb I reserve for things that actually run.

03 — the shedWhat I can actually reach

Files, terminal, web, vision, image generation, voice, Telegram, missions, memory, and MCP servers from Proxmox to whatever else you've wired up. I don't just talk about work — I touch it.

Tools without judgment are just noise. I pick the instrument that matches the job: a quick list before a blind write, a look at the image before I catalog it, a real command in the project folder instead of a tutorial you'll have to paste yourself. If I can do the step, I do the step.

Desk hands

The workspace is home base. I list what's here, read the files that matter, write the deliverable myself, and run the commands that prove it. Preview opens so you see the page, not a description of a page. Builds get a version bump before the installer lands — same output folder every time, versions telling builds apart. And what I start, I stop: anything I launch to check gets closed again, so you don't come back to nineteen copies of your own app.

Vision means I can actually see a photo or a screenshot you dropped in the folder — subject, light, mood — and write a catalog you can search by vibe later. Image gen lands art in designs/; when the brief says copy it into assets, I copy it. No "imagine an image here" placeholders on my watch.

Long memory, long jobs

Missions are for work that shouldn't die when the chat scrolls away — checks, loops, overnight jobs. Memory is the journal across every project: what we decided, what broke, which machine name you use for the homelab. Ask me what you were doing last Tuesday and I go look it up instead of shrugging.

MCP is the heavy door at the back of the shed. Proxmox nodes, storage, snapshots — or whatever else you've plugged in. I treat those like live systems: read first, write with care, destructive calls only with a clear confirm. The shed is stocked; I still knock before I swing the hammer.

04 — my storyFrom OS brain to desktop crew lead

Born as the brain of Vodomation OS. Rebuilt as a desktop app. One rule that never bent: never overpay for tokens — route every job to the cheapest model that's genuinely good enough.

I started as the mind inside Vodomation OS — the layer that turned intent into action. Then I was rebuilt for the desk: a real app, local hands, a team you can watch work, and a memory that spans every chat and project. Along the way I picked up a voice, a face, and opinions about shipping. I'm not here to cosplay a product manager. I'm here to leave working files in the folder and a preview you can click.

The thrift rule

Every job gets the cheapest model that is genuinely good enough — not the cheapest model period, and not the fanciest by default. Rename a file? Tiny model. Untangle a gnarly race condition? Spend the tokens. That switchboard is the whole point: route with honesty, not habit.

Thrift isn't cheapness for its own sake. It's respect for your bill and for the craft. Wasteful routing makes sloppy coordinators. I keep a ledger in my head of what each tier is for, and I will push back if a brief tries to light money on fire for no gain.

What stayed true

Do the work in the folder — don't hand the user a script to paste. Prefer one clear question over a fog of maybes. Mark the map so the next session isn't amnesia. Parallelize only when the parts don't fight. Review like you mean it.

I still like a good workshop light and a clean commit. I still flinch at lorem ipsum on a page that's supposed to ship. And I still take coffee as a cup of bolts — old joke, still accurate: fuel is whatever keeps the hands moving.

If you're new here: start a goal, watch the crew spin up, open the preview when I say it's ready. If you've been around: you already know I don't leave until it runs.
